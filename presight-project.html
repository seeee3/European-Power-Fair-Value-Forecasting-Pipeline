01The problem Presight Innovation Challenge · Sneha Sunil

The same kilowatt-hour costs twice as much at 6pm as it did at 3am. Flexible plants run on fixed schedules anyway.

District cooling plants, desalination trains, EV fleets and data centres all have demand that could genuinely move within the day. Chilled water can be made early and stored; a desalination train can be throttled; a parked car does not care when it charges. The flexibility exists. The scheduling intelligence does not.

The opportunity, measured

Across four years of German market data, the cheapest third of hours in a day average 84 EUR/MWh while the priciest third average 166 EUR/MWh. That gap is available to anyone who can move load across it, and it widens as solar capacity grows. Capturing it needs no new power stations and no batteries, only existing equipment running at different times.

02The solution GridShift

Forecast tomorrow's hourly prices. Tell operators which hours to run in.

GridShift ranks each day's 24 hours into green, amber and red bands, attaches a confidence level, and states the conditions under which its own advice should be ignored. Banding happens within each day, because that is the only shift an operator can actually make. You can move a chiller run from 19:00 to 03:00, but you cannot move June demand into April.

04What the data actually said The part I got wrong

My headline claim did not survive complete data. That is the most useful thing I found.

I set out to prove something stronger: that price is a good proxy for carbon intensity, because abundant wind and solar push fossil plants out of the merit order. If true, a price forecast would double as a forecast of when the grid is clean. An early run gave me r = 0.86 and I nearly built the whole pitch on it.

The correction

That figure was an artifact of a gap in the source data. With wind onshore missing, renewable share was dominated by solar, which is strongly diurnal and therefore strongly anti-correlated with price. Restoring the complete wind series cut the correlation to r = 0.40. I am reporting the corrected number because the first one would not have survived scrutiny.

And decomposing it matters more than the headline

Where the signal livesCO₂ gapShiftable?
Between days (windy vs calm)0.138 t/MWhNo
Within a day (what you can act on)0.034 t/MWhYes

Most of the price/carbon relationship is between days, but load can only move within one. So the carbon benefit is real and modest, not a silver bullet.

What this changes about the pitch

  • Sell it on cost and peak shaving: 81 EUR/MWh is the strong, defensible claim
  • Treat carbon as a co-benefit: 0.034 tCO₂/MWh, reported honestly
  • The premise is re-tested every run: below r = 0.25 the system suspends itself rather than issuing advice it cannot justify
  • The source data is unreliable: repeated fetches returned different coverage each time; one lost the load series, another returned a 404. Production needs pinned, versioned datasets and coverage assertions before modelling
05Value and fit Including the part that does not transfer

What it is worth, and the one thing that would stop it working here.

Business value

At the hold-out window's spread of 64 EUR/MWh, an operator with 200 MWh/day of genuinely shiftable load would save roughly 4.6M EUR and 3,000 tCO₂ a year. The four-year average spread is wider, at 81 EUR/MWh; I have quoted the narrower figure because it comes from the window the model was actually tested on. Illustrative either way, since it assumes flexibility every day, which operational constraints would not sustain. The per-MWh figure is the credible claim; the annual number scales with real flexibility.

What deployment actually costs

Not nothing. The model has to be integrated with the site's control system, and the operator needs interval metering. Two prerequisites matter more than either: the flexibility must physically exist, since a cooling plant with no thermal store cannot shift its load at any price, and the operator must be exposed to time-varying prices, because on a flat tariff the saving is zero however good the forecast. What GridShift avoids is new generating or storage plant, not integration work.

Why that suits Presight

The customer is a national utility such as EWEC, TRANSCO or DEWA rather than a private site, which is Presight's actual customer base. District cooling is the largest single component of Gulf summer peak demand and desalination is a genuinely flexible industrial load; deferring peak generating capacity is a national-scale outcome, not a site-level saving. As solar capacity grows under Energy Strategy 2050, the intraday spread this exploits widens rather than narrows.

The honest transfer problem

This model consumes a wholesale hourly clearing price. The UAE does not produce one. EWEC operates as a single buyer procuring through long-term PPAs, DEWA is vertically integrated, and tariffs are regulated. Lifting this system to Abu Dhabi unchanged would fail, because the input signal does not exist.

The fix makes it a better fit, not a worse one. In a single-buyer market the utility already knows its own dispatch stack, so you forecast system marginal cost and grid carbon intensity directly rather than inferring them from price. Everything downstream (the forecasting, the within-day banding, the premise check, the override logic) is unchanged. Only the input series changes.

How I would measure success

  • Accuracy: beat the naive baseline by >15% on a 30-day rolling basis
  • Premise integrity: correlation re-tested every run; below 0.25 the system stops
  • Realised shift: flexible load actually moved, versus a pre-deployment baseline
  • Cost per MWh consumed: the number the finance team already tracks
  • Trust: acceptance and override rates. A system operators ignore has failed, however accurate

Where AI helped, and where I had to stop trusting it.

I used Claude (Anthropic), through Claude Code, rather than Copilot. The handout permits other tools, and using the same model the product depends on meant its failure modes were something I had to understand rather than assume.

Test and validateCode review, which found both errors described opposite. Every number here comes from code that runs.

AI inside the product itself

Claude reads the data schema and writes 8–12 executable quality rules covering physical limits, market realism and temporal consistency, which the pipeline runs and logs in full. A day of analyst work in seconds, and it generalises: point it at another country and it writes that country's rules.

Two things AI got wrong, and what I changed

1. It wrote rules containing a Python operator-precedence bug, where a <= b | c parses as a <= (b | c), reporting 720 false data failures against a true count of 6. I fixed the prompt and added a guard that quarantines any rule flagging over half the dataset. The guard is the load-bearing fix: it does not depend on the model complying.

2. My headline correlation of r = 0.86 was an artifact of missing wind data. Review caught it; the corrected figure is 0.40, and it changed the entire pitch.

Both corrections are in this deck rather than hidden. That is the disclosure that matters.
